SIGNAL TESTING

Does buying fear actually work?

Two gauges claim to spot the moments when fear is a buying opportunity: CNN’s Fear & Greed Index and our own BATS score. We ran the same tests on both, over the same 15+ years of S&P 500 history, and put the results side by side. Every rule we ran is on this page, including the ones that lost.

Test 1 — What happened after a low reading

The simplest test, with no selling and no rules. Take every day a gauge was low and look at where the S&P 500 stood later. If a gauge is any good, stocks should have done better than usual after its low readings.

And after a high reading? Did stocks do worse?

This is the reverse question. If a high reading were a reason to sell, stocks should have done worse than usual afterwards. So compare each number with the ordinary day in the bottom row. A number below it means the warning came true. A number at or above it means there was no warning at all.

Test 2 — Trading rules, side by side

Now turn each gauge into a rule that buys and sells, and compare it with simply holding. Each rule below is run twice, once on each gauge, so the two columns are always the same idea.

How a rule can win its trades and still earn less. A rule only earns while it is invested, and these rules sit in cash most of the time. So read the rows in two groups.

“Trades won” and “average gain per trade” tell you how good the signal was. “$1 grew to” tells you how the whole plan did, waiting time included. A gauge can win the first group and lose the second.

“Gain per year while invested” joins the two. It asks how fast the money grew during the time it was actually in stocks, which is the fair way to compare a rule that is invested half the time with one that is invested a fifth of the time.

MACD crossover signals

The buy and sell signals on the Divergences page, run against S&P 500 history: buy when the MACD line (12, 26) crosses above its 9-period signal line after at least 10 closed bars below it, sell on the mirror-image cross after at least 10 bars above. The position changes at the close of the bar the cross is first visible on, which is the same information as acting at the next bar’s open. Three variants sit alongside it: the same cross with no 10-bar minimum, the 10-bar rule with a 200-day trend filter, and the 10-bar rule on weekly bars.
